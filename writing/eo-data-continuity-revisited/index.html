Australia’s EO data continuity risks, two years on

6 November 2026 · George Dyke · aeo-forum-2026 earth-observation data-continuity

Australia gets an extraordinary amount of value from satellites that we don’t own. That has been true for decades, and on balance it has worked remarkably well. But being able to download data today and being confident of access in ten years’ time are different things.

In 2024, a Continuity of Earth Observation Data for Australia (CEODA) assessment set out 39 risks to the supply of satellite Earth observation (EO) data for Australia. I’ve been going back through those risks while preparing a talk for the 2026 Australian Earth Observation Forum in Hobart. My quick assessment is that the headline risk ratings have moved very little, despite quite a lot happening in the world around them.

Note: I have contributed to several earlier CEODA studies, but this is my own reading of the situation, not a position endorsed by the organisations involved in past CEODA studies. The 2026 comparison is also a provisional update, rather than a new, formally agreed assessment.

The 2024 baseline

The 2024 report cited a Deloitte estimate of A$283 million in direct economic contribution from satellite EO in 2020, and more than A$2.5 billion in economic activity supported each year. It also counted 171 government programs that depend on EO, with 160 (93%) rated as having medium-to-high dependency. Those are substantial numbers for a country whose underlying satellite data comes overwhelmingly from overseas.

The 2024 report outlined 39 risks ranging from government policy and mission programs to ground infrastructure, processing, archives and the arrangements by which Australian users actually obtain data. Revisiting the list: which risks actually look different now, and what does that tell us about Australia’s options?

Stable ratings, changing pressures

Of the 39 risks, my provisional view is that 15 have become more pressing, 18 are broadly unchanged and six have eased. “More pressing” means that developments since 2024 suggest a greater likelihood of disruption, potentially greater consequences, or less confidence in the arrangements that protect continuity. “Eased” means those pressures appear to have reduced; it does not mean the risk has disappeared. These judgments describe the direction of change, rather than necessarily a change in the overall risk rating.

This is not an attempt to repeat the original assessment process, but rather is a review of what has happened since, using the same risks as a reference point. Some judgments have better evidence behind them than others. And a risk that stays in the same category can still be moving in an uncomfortable direction.

Most of these movements are not large enough to change the overall risk rating. The exception, in my view, is PR4: changes in foreign governments’ priorities for their space agency programmes, illustrated by the reworking of Landsat Next. I would now rate this risk High rather than Medium, reflecting greater uncertainty about the future capabilities Australia can rely on. The other 38 risks would retain their 2024 ratings.

I don’t think the story is that overseas governments are walking away from EO. Investment is still substantial, and in some areas growing, including in the US and Europe. What has changed is what governments want from that investment. Security, domestic industry, and strategic autonomy have become much more prominent. That can create new capabilities, but it can also change which missions get funded, what gets built and how the data is supplied.

Landsat: partnership in a changing program

You can see the change in several places: the reconsideration of the US Landsat architecture, ESA’s European Resilience from Space initiative, and the greater attention to security and industry in UK and Japanese space policy. These aren’t all the same sort of policy change, of course. What they share is that decisions being made elsewhere have real consequences for the data Australia expects to use.

Landsat is a useful example. The Australia–US partnership announced in 2024 was a significant step, strengthening Australia’s contribution to the programme and its position in discussions about future capabilities and access. The subsequent US reworking of the future Landsat programme illustrates the wider context in which these partnerships operate: mission plans remain subject to evolving priorities and funding decisions in the country leading the programme.

For Australia, this reinforces the value of sustained engagement with partners such as the USGS. These relationships give us opportunities to shape outcomes and respond to change. They are central to managing continuity risk, while some decisions affecting the data we rely on will inevitably remain outside Australia’s control.

And a shift towards security and industry is not necessarily bad news for EO users. It may bring more money and better systems. The uncertainty is in the priorities attached to that money. Those priorities can change program timetables, instrument choices, procurement and access policies, even when overall spending is holding up.

A concrete example of the geopolitical-conflict risk (PO9) came in April 2026, when Planet Labs announced it would withhold imagery of Iran and the wider Middle East conflict zone following a US government request. While the satellites were still operating, this demonstrated that continuity of access can be impacted.

Two emerging concerns

Two further issues deserve attention. These are separate from the original 39 risks and don’t have assigned ratings at present.

First, deliberate interference with space systems. This is hardly a new idea, but counterspace activity has become harder to dismiss as a remote possibility. As satellite systems become more central to governments, economies and defence, they become more important targets too. Disruption might be directed at a spacecraft, a radio link or a ground system. From the perspective of someone relying on the data, the result could look much the same: supply stops or becomes unreliable.

In October 2026, Russia’s Kosmos 2612 conducted a series of close manoeuvres around the commercial radar satellite ICEYE-X36, illustrating the potential exposure of civilian EO systems to such activity.

Second, frontier AI. I don’t think anyone can confidently tell us where this ends up. Will it mainly make existing EO work cheaper and faster, or create new applications and markets that weren’t practical before? Probably some of both. But a growing share of the models, computing infrastructure and services involved in that change sits with foreign companies. If those services become part of our routine EO processing, they become part of the dependency story as well.

That isn’t a case for avoiding AI. It is a case for asking fairly practical questions: can we move a workflow to a different model or provider, can we recover the data and code, and what happens if access terms change suddenly?

What genuine resilience looks like

To start with, genuinely usable alternatives. If one mission or provider becomes unavailable, can we switch to another without rebuilding an entire application? This isn’t simply a matter of listing possible satellites. Resolution, calibration, coverage, timeliness, licences and processing all determine whether a substitute is actually useful.

Then there is the less visible institutional side. Have we funded the processing, workflows, and archives to switch supply? Are access arrangements dependable? Is there a replacement mission on a real budget, rather than just in a strategy document? And do Australian agencies and companies still have the expertise to operate the systems they depend on?

Australia also has ways of improving its position that don’t involve owning a satellite. Our work through the Committee on Earth Observation Satellites (CEOS), particularly in calibration and validation and in shaping common specifications such as CEOS Analysis Ready Data, is one example. It means we have some say in the technical practices that make the global system useful to us.

There is a practical benefit here too. Good calibration facilities, reference measurements and common standards make it easier to compare data from different missions and, when necessary, switch between them. That is a real contribution to continuity. It is not as visible as a launch, but it matters.

Sovereign options along the whole chain

I do think Australia should have a serious conversation about national EO missions, drawing on the experience of the cancelled National Space Mission for Earth Observation (NSMEO). That includes what a national mission could contribute to data continuity, and what sustained commitment would be needed to deliver it. Sovereign capability also extends beyond the spacecraft itself: data reception, archives, Cal/Val infrastructure, processing and technical expertise can all give Australia options it would otherwise lack.

We don’t need to own every part of the chain, and I doubt that would be sensible. We will keep relying on international partners. The question is which dependencies we are comfortable carrying, which ones we can reduce through cooperation, and where we need a capability of our own.

After going back through the 2024 assessment, my main conclusion isn’t that the old risk picture was wrong. Quite the opposite: the developments since 2024 reinforce the relevance of the original assessment. What has changed is the domestic and international political and industrial context in which decisions about EO are being made. Those decisions will continue to be made largely outside Australia, however close our partnerships are.

So rather than asking only how many satellites are available to us, perhaps we should ask a harder question: what does Australia need to control, what can we meaningfully influence, and what must we be able to replace?
