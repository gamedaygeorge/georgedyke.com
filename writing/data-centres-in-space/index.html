Data centres in space: the bigger question for Earth observation

9 November 2026 · George Dyke · aeo-forum-2026 earth-observation space-infrastructure

This piece grew out of preparing a talk for the Advancing Earth Observation Forum 2026 in Hobart. It is a think piece, not a long-considered thesis or a forecast: an effort to explore an idea that I suspect deserves more attention.

There’s a lively debate about whether data centres in space make technical or commercial sense. I’m not going to try to settle it.

What interests me is a different question: what might the pursuit of orbital computing mean for Earth observation, whether or not orbital data centres succeed as a business?

I think the EO community is paying too little attention to what is happening in the adjacent world of low Earth orbit communications and computing.

The infrastructure may be the story

Rather than imagining a terrestrial server warehouse launched into orbit, picture a network of increasingly capable spacecraft consisting of interconnected nodes with computing, storage, communications, power and thermal-management systems.

Communications constellations already offer a glimpse of this architecture. The ambition around orbital computing could push it much further, drawing on investment and engineering capacity well beyond what the EO community could readily mobilise on its own.

That doesn’t mean every orbital computing venture will succeed, or that EO will automatically benefit. But ambitious – even speculative – investment can accelerate technologies and expertise that outlive the original business proposition. The history of infrastructure booms offers examples, although space hardware is not as easily repurposed as terrestrial fibre or railway lines.

A camera on every satellite?

As a deliberately simple thought experiment, imagine a modest smartphone-class camera on every spacecraft in a constellation the size of Starlink. What new observations might become possible with so many potential viewpoints?

Of course, a camera is not an EO mission. Pointing, optics, illumination, calibration, coverage and data quality all matter. This thought experiment is not a plan for a scientific observing system.

The more interesting possibility is that communications spacecraft increasingly become sensing platforms, and EO spacecraft increasingly use infrastructure they don’t need to carry themselves.

What if satellites followed the PC?

Personal computing went from relatively standalone machines to an ecosystem of desktops, laptops, smartphones, servers, cloud services, watches, and cars. These devices have different strengths, but common interfaces, software and networks let them work together.

Perhaps spacecraft could develop along a similar path. We would still need specialised instruments, from large optical and radar systems to much smaller cameras and sensors. But the platforms hosting them might become increasingly adaptable and interconnected.

Imagine launching a satellite with the orbital equivalent of a network card: it joins a network and can use communications, processing or storage services elsewhere, rather than bringing every capability along for the ride.

This may be a more fundamental restructure than just faster downlinks or tip-and-cue model, and suggests a different way of designing spacecraft and assembling EO capabilities, not simply coordinating the missions we already know how to build.

There are substantial obstacles, from interoperability and security to business models and orbital sustainability. It may also reinforce dependence on even fewer infrastructure providers: if EO comes to rely on networks it neither owns nor governs, the terms of access become someone else’s decision.

I think the EO community could be asking what role it wants in shaping this emerging infrastructure, rather than waiting to see whether the orbital data-centre market takes off.

Links and further reading

For a closer look at the technical and commercial feasibility debate, these three analyses offer different perspectives:

  • Boston Consulting Group (August 2026), “Space-Based Data Centers: More Than Hype, but Not a Revolution” – a strategic and economic assessment, including potential space-specific uses.
  • Avi Parrack and Fin Moorhouse, Forethought (May 2026), “Will We Really Put Data Centers in Space?” – detailed technical and economic modelling of the feasibility case.
  • Iris ten Have, Rate Limit (May 2026), “Orbital data centers are like Dubai chocolate” – a sceptical look at the engineering challenges and economics.

For background on the historical parallels:

  • PBS American Experience, “The Panic of 1873” – the US railway construction boom, financial crash and extensive track network built along the way.
  • EDUCAUSE Review, “Lessons for the Future Internet: Learning from the Past” – how dot-com-era investment in fibre networks helped enable later internet services.

For examples of faster satellite data delivery:

  • SpaceNews (2 November 2026), “Vantor cuts satellite imagery delivery time to seven minutes in Ukraine test” – a reported tasking-to-tablet demonstration.
  • Airbus (1 June 2016), “SpaceDataHighway: first laser transmission of an image taken by the Sentinel 1A satellite” – Copernicus Sentinel-1A images reached the receiving ground facility in about one minute via EDRS laser relay.

Examples of enabling technology:

  • Australian Space Agency – Spiral Blue’s SE-1 space-edge computer demonstrates Australian experience with onboard processing.
  • Google (October 2026) – Project Suncatcher’s orbital TPU test illustrates the ambitions and open engineering questions of orbital computing.
  • Starlink – Mini Laser technology is designed to connect third-party spacecraft to its optical relay network, an early example of potential shared infrastructure.
